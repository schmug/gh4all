# How to Use These Documents (No Git Experience Needed)

These documents live on GitHub so they can be **public, version-tracked, and improved by the people who use them**.

You do **not** need to be a programmer, and you do **not** need to install anything. Everything below happens in your web browser. The only thing you'll need is a **free GitHub account** — and even that is only required if you want to *comment* or *suggest changes*. Reading is open to everyone.

> **What you can do here**
> - 📖 Read the documents
> - 🕓 See the full history of any document — who changed what, and when
> - 💬 Ask a question or report a problem
> - ✏️ Suggest a specific change to the wording
> - 📋 Make your own copy to customize for your own use

---

## A few words you'll see, in plain English

GitHub uses some unfamiliar vocabulary. Here's all you really need:

- **Repository (or "repo")** — The whole collection of documents, plus the record of every change ever made to them. Think of it as a shared binder that remembers its own edit history.
- **GitHub** — The website that hosts the binder (you're on it right now).
- **Commit** — A single saved change, with a short note describing what was changed. Like a "save point" in Track Changes.
- **History** — The list of every commit on a document: who, what, and when.
- **Issue** — A message you post to start a conversation: report a problem, ask a question, or request a change. Like opening a help-desk ticket or leaving a comment.
- **Pull request** — A specific edit you're proposing and asking our team to review and accept. Like submitting suggested edits for approval.
- **Merge** — When our team accepts a proposed change into the official documents.
- **Fork** — Your *own complete copy* of this binder, under your account, that you can change however you like. Like photocopying the whole binder so you can mark up your own version.
- **Markdown** — The simple text format these documents are written in. You don't have to learn it to read them, and you can pick up the basics in a minute if you ever edit one.

That's it. You can ignore everything else.

## 1. Read a document

1. Browse the folders and click any file ending in `.md` to open it.
2. It displays as a normal, formatted page — no special software needed.
3. To save or print, use your browser's print/save feature, or click the **Download raw file** button (the download icon near the top of the file).

---

## 2. See the history of a document

1. Open the document.
2. Click the **History** button (near the top-right of the file).
3. You'll see every change, newest first, with the date and the person who made it.
4. Click any entry to see *exactly* what changed — added text appears in green, removed text in red.

This is how you can always tell what's current, what changed recently, and why.

---

## 3. Ask a question or report a problem

Use this when something is unclear, out of date, or wrong, but you don't want to rewrite it yourself.

1. Click the **Issues** tab at the top of the repository.
2. Click **New issue**.
3. Give it a short **title** and describe the question or problem in the box.
4. Click **Submit new issue**.

Our team will see it and respond in the thread. *(Requires a free GitHub account.)*

---

## 4. Suggest a specific change to the wording

Use this when you know exactly what you'd like a document to say.

1. Open the document and click the **pencil (Edit) icon** near the top-right.
2. Because you don't have edit access to the official copy, GitHub will automatically make a personal copy for you — just accept when it offers. *(This is the "fork" step, handled for you.)*
3. Make your edits directly in the text.
4. Click **Commit changes…**, then write a one-line note describing what you changed.
5. Click **Propose changes**, then **Create pull request**.

Your suggestion goes to our team to review. We may accept it, ask questions, or suggest adjustments — all in the same place. Nothing changes in the official document until we accept it.

---

## 5. Make your own copy to customize for your own use

Use this when you want to adapt a document for local use — for example, dropping your organization's name, contacts, and procedures into a template.

1. Click the **Fork** button (near the top-right of the repository).
2. You now have your own complete copy under your own account or organization.
3. Edit it however you like — it's yours. Your changes do **not** affect the official version.
4. *(Optional, more advanced)* You can pull in future updates we make to the original later on.

---

## Frequently asked

**Do I need to install Git or use a command line?**
No. Everything above is done in your browser.

**Do I need an account?**
Only to comment, suggest changes, or make a copy. Reading requires nothing.

**Will I break something?**
No. You cannot change the official documents directly. Anything you propose is reviewed first, and your own forked copy is completely separate.

**These are templates — can I just use them as-is?**
They're living references meant to be adapted to your local context. Review and adjust anything before adopting it for your organization.
